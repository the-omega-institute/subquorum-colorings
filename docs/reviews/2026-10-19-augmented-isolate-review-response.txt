The witness argument works without accounting assumptionsWrite  $a(v)=|N(v)\cap S|$ . The witness classifications used in the note follow directly from minimality and bipartiteness.If  $h\in H$  is deleted,  $h$  itself remains formed: its at least two selected neighbors survive. Therefore a deletion failure occurs at a vertex  $x$  outside the original  $S$ .If  $x$  is adjacent to  $h$ , failure after deleting  $h$  forces
$$N(x)\cap S=\{h\}
\quad\text{or}\quad
N(x)\cap S=\{h,i\}.$$
In the second case,  $i$  must have no selected neighbor after the deletion. Both  $h$  and  $i$  lie opposite  $x$ , so bipartiteness makes them nonadjacent. Consequently  $i$  was already a selected isolate.If  $x$  is nonadjacent to  $h$ , its selected-neighbor set is unchanged by deleting  $h$ . Failure therefore requires
$$N(x)\cap S=\{g_h\},
\qquad
N(g_h)\cap S=\{h\}.$$
Thus  $g_h$  is a leaf supported by  $h$ .These descriptions also justify injectivity: a high-failure witness determines its high owner uniquely. In the adjacent cases its selected neighborhood identifies the high owner; in the nonadjacent case its sole selected neighbor identifies its unique high support.The corresponding leaf statement also holds at arbitrary degree. If  $u\in U$  has high support  $h$ , deleting  $u$  leaves  $u$  formed through  $h$ , because  $h$  retains another selected neighbor. A failure therefore occurs outside the original  $S$ . A nonadjacent failure would require a selected vertex whose sole selected neighbor is  $u$ . The only selected neighbor of  $u$  is its high support  $h$ , which has at least two selected neighbors. Hence the failure witness is adjacent, with
$$N(x_u)\cap S=\{u\}
\quad\text{or}\quad
N(x_u)\cap S=\{u,i_u\},
